# For physicists

Every notebook here is self-contained and meant to be opened in Google Colab; see the [repository README](../README.md) for how to run one in class. Each entry below says what the student is asked to write and which part of the Z3 primer the notebook assumes.

The selling point across this folder is that the textbook equations go in unrearranged. The student never has to solve for the unknown first, or pick the clever point to take torques about; they state what they know and ask for what they do not.

You can find a number of Jupyter notebooks for:

- Mechanics:
  + Work out what happens when two carts collide [link](COLLISIONS.ipynb). <details><summary>Click to expand description</summary> Most of the work in an introductory mechanics problem is algebra: you know which conservation laws apply, then you rearrange them until the unknown is alone. Here conservation of energy and of momentum are stated to Z3 exactly as they are written in a textbook, the known quantities are fixed, and the solver finds the rest. The notebook builds from kinetic and potential energy to a perfectly inelastic collision, and then asks whether kinetic energy is conserved in that collision too. The answer is "unsat": nothing was computed and nothing was compared, the claim was simply incompatible with the physics already in the solver. The carts are drawn before and after. <br><br> <b>Student task:</b> state conservation of kinetic energy for the elastic case, and read off what happens to the two carts. <br> <b>Z3 primer:</b> reals.</details>

  + Solve projectile motion problems [link](PROJECTILE-MOTION.ipynb). <details><summary>Click to expand description</summary> The kinematic equations describe a projectile completely, but using them means picking the right one for the unknown you want and rearranging it. This notebook gives Z3 all of the equations at once instead and asks for whichever quantity is missing. Sines and cosines are not something Z3 knows about, so the notebook shows one way of supplying them, and the launch velocity is split using the known launch angle. The horizontal and vertical motion are set up separately and then wrapped in a small class so that any projectile problem can be posed to it; a cannonball fired from a building and a kicked soccer ball are both solved, and the trajectory is plotted. <br><br> <b>Student task:</b> complete the vertical kinematic equations, split the launch velocity into its vertical component, and state the condition that holds at the apex of the flight. <br> <b>Z3 primer:</b> reals.</details>

  + Find the forces holding up a loaded beam [link](STATIC-EQUILIBRIUM.ipynb). <details><summary>Click to expand description</summary> Working out how hard a support pushes on a loaded beam normally means choosing a clever point to take torques about so that the unknowns you do not care about drop out. Here the force balance and the torque balance are both written down in full, every term kept, and Z3 finds the unknown reactions. The notebook starts with a symmetric beam whose answer can be checked by eye, then removes one support and moves the weight off-centre: the forces can still be made to cancel, but the torque condition cannot be met, and the solver returns "unsat". <br><br> <b>Student task:</b> write the torque balance for a beam carrying both its own weight and an off-centre load. <br> <b>Z3 primer:</b> reals.</details>

- Electricity:
  + Analyze circuits, from Ohm's law to RC charging [link](RC-CIRCUIT.ipynb). <details><summary>Click to expand description</summary> A circuit problem is a set of simultaneous relationships: Ohm's law at every resistor, Kirchhoff's laws at every junction and around every loop, and an exponential for the capacitor. The notebook states all of them at once and then asks for whichever quantity is missing, building from a single resistor through series and parallel networks to a charging RC circuit. Z3 has no exponential of its own, so the notebook shows one way of supplying it. <br><br> <b>Student task:</b> hand the RC circuit the equivalent resistance of the two parallel resistors, then read off the time at which the capacitor reaches a given voltage. <br> <b>Z3 primer:</b> reals. <br> <i>The same notebook also appears in <a href="../For%20engineers/README.md">For engineers</a>.</i></details>

- Optics:
  + Locate the image formed by a curved mirror [link](OPTICS.ipynb). <details><summary>Click to expand description</summary> Working out where a curved mirror puts an image usually means rearranging the mirror equation for whichever quantity was not given and then consulting a table of sign conventions to say what the answer means. Here the whole relationship goes to Z3 unrearranged, cleared of fractions so that nothing divides by an unknown. The notebook solves for the focal length of a convex mirror, adds magnification to get the image height and orientation, and then places an object exactly at the focal point of a concave mirror and gets "unsat" — there is no image distance at all, which is the physics rather than a failure of the solver. <br><br> <b>Student task:</b> write the mirror equation for a concave mirror with a given focal length, object distance and object height. <br> <b>Z3 primer:</b> reals. <br> <i>The same notebook also appears in <a href="../For%20engineers/README.md">For engineers</a>.</i></details>
