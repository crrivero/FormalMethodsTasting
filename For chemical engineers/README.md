# For chemical engineers

Every notebook here is self-contained and meant to be opened in Google Colab; see the [repository README](../README.md) for how to run one in class. Each entry below says what the student is asked to write and which part of the Z3 primer the notebook assumes.

These notebooks assume little programming background. The molecule or the reaction is what the student thinks about; Z3 is the thing that answers. Nothing the student edits involves a loop, a comprehension or a list index, except in the advanced reaction notebook, which says so.

You can find a number of Jupyter notebooks for:

- Reaction stoichiometry:
  + Balance a chemical reaction [link](REACTION.ipynb). <details><summary>Click to expand description</summary> Balancing a reaction by hand means juggling one equation per element until the coefficients work out, and those equations are exactly the kind of thing a solver is good at. The notebook gives each compound an integer coefficient, writes one balance equation per element, and requires every coefficient to be positive. It starts with the formation of water, where the student writes a single equation, and finishes with the combustion of propanol, where three elements mean three equations. <br><br> <b>Student task:</b> write the balance equation for hydrogen in the water reaction, then all three balance equations for the combustion of propanol. <br> <b>Z3 primer:</b> integers.</details>

  + Balance <i>any</i> chemical reaction [link](REACTION-ADVANCED.ipynb). <details><summary>Click to expand description</summary> The sequel to the notebook above. Writing one equation per element by hand works, but it means starting over for every new reaction. Here the student writes a single program that takes a reaction given as a dictionary of molecules and their elements, generates the coefficients and the balance equations itself, and hands the whole system to Z3. It is tested on the water reaction and then on the combustion of propanol. This is the one notebook in the folder that expects a little comfort with dictionaries and loops. <br><br> <b>Student task:</b> create a Z3 variable for each coefficient, build the per-element totals, equate the two sides of the reaction, and then write out the propanol combustion as input to the finished function. <br> <b>Z3 primer:</b> integers.</details>

  + Determine the limiting reactant [link](LIMITING-REACTANT.ipynb). <details><summary>Click to expand description</summary> For the combustion of propane, given starting amounts of each reactant, which one runs out first and how far does the reaction get? The notebook introduces a variable for the extent of the reaction, ties the amount of each reactant consumed and each product formed to that extent through the stoichiometric coefficients, and constrains consumption not to exceed what was supplied. Finding the answer means pushing the extent as far as it will go, so the notebook uses Z3's optimizer rather than a plain solver. <br><br> <b>Student task:</b> write the consumption and production relationships for the remaining species, and the constraint that no more of a reactant can be used than was available. <br> <b>Z3 primer:</b> integers.</details>

- Molecular structure:
  + Draw a valid Lewis structure [link](LEWIS-STRUCTURES.ipynb). <details><summary>Click to expand description</summary> Drawing a Lewis structure means distributing the valence electrons as bond pairs and lone pairs so that every atom ends up with a full shell. Those are arithmetic constraints on integers, one per rule: all valence electrons are distributed, every atom has four pairs in total, every bond is at least a single bond, and no count is negative. The notebook works through carbon dioxide first, using Z3's optimizer to pick the resonance structure with the smallest formal charge, and then hands the student nitrogen triiodide to encode from scratch. The solution is drawn as a structure, with the caveat that the bond angles are not physically meaningful. <br><br> <b>Student task:</b> the most heavily scaffolded set in the project — every marked line is one expression about valence electrons or bond pairs, first for carbon dioxide and then for the whole nitrogen triiodide solver. <br> <b>Z3 primer:</b> integers.</details>

  + Compute the Z index of a molecule [link](Z-INDEX.ipynb). <details><summary>Click to expand description</summary> Two molecules built from the same atoms can boil at different temperatures, because what differs is how the atoms are connected. The Z index captures part of that: it counts the matchings in a molecule's chemical graph, and it tracks the boiling point closely enough to be useful. Each bond becomes a boolean variable, the constraints say that no carbon atom is in two selected bonds at once, and every solution is enumerated and drawn. The notebook does this for three isomers of hexane and then lines the three Z indices up against their measured boiling points, showing that the less branched molecules score higher. <br><br> <b>Student task:</b> write the "at most one selected bond" constraint for each carbon atom in the second and third isomers. <br> <b>Z3 primer:</b> booleans.</details>
